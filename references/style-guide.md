## Chapter 9 — Grammar, Punctuation, and Spelling

### Extracted guidance

- **Make the verb agree with its actual subject.** Intervening phrases can hide a singular subject. Two subjects joined by *and* usually take a plural verb; with *or*, agreement follows the nearer subject. A number of samples *were* measured, but the number of samples *was* recorded. Units used as a single quantity normally take a singular verb. The guide allows *data* as singular when referring to a collection and plural when referring to individual results. Scientific fields such as *kinetics* are generally singular. (pp. 105–109)
- **Distinguish essential information from extra information.** A restrictive clause identifies which item is meant and ordinarily uses *that* without surrounding commas. A nonrestrictive clause adds supplementary information and uses *which* or *who* with commas. Removing the clause is a useful test. (p. 109)
- **Attach introductory modifiers to the right subject.** In a sentence beginning "After stirring the mixture, …", the grammatical subject must be whoever stirred it. Recast the opening if the subject is the mixture or another object. The guide uses *based on* to modify a noun and *on the basis of* to modify an action; it reserves *due to* for the sense "attributable to" and uses *because of* or *resulted from* elsewhere. (pp. 109–113)
- **Use commas to show sentence structure.** Put a comma after most introductory phrases and before the conjunction joining two independent clauses. Use the serial comma in lists of three or more items. Do not separate a verb from its subject or object with a comma, and do not add a comma between two parts of a compound predicate. (pp. 113–118)
- **Choose punctuation by its function.** A semicolon joins closely related independent clauses or separates list items that already contain commas. A colon introduces an explanation or list after a grammatically complete lead-in; do not put one immediately after *are*, *include*, or a preposition. Parentheses contain subsidiary information. The guide uses logical quotation punctuation: a mark goes inside the quotation marks only if it belongs to the quoted material. (pp. 119–124)
- **Differentiate hyphens, en dashes, and em dashes.** An en dash links equally weighted concepts (*dose–response*) and spans (*Figures 1–4*); write *from 1 to 4* and *between 1 and 4*. For a range with a negative value or a modifying symbol, spell out *to*. An em dash sets off a potentially ambiguous interruption, but ordinary commas are preferred when they suffice. Hyphenation is covered more fully in Chapter 10. (pp. 124–127)
- **Use consistent American spelling.** Consult a dictionary for uncertain words and the chapter's spelling appendix for preferred variants. Check easily confused singular and plural forms, such as *criterion/criteria*, *phenomenon/phenomena*, and *spectrum/spectra*. (pp. 127–133)

**In practice:** Check each sentence for the true subject, the referent of its opening phrase, and whether a comma changes the meaning of a *that/which* clause. These checks are especially useful when writing experimental methods and figure descriptions.

## Chapter 10 — Editorial Style

### Extracted guidance

- **Hyphenate for meaning and consistency.** Most ordinary prefixes and suffixes attach without hyphens, with exceptions where pronunciation or meaning requires separation. A two-word modifier before a noun commonly takes a hyphen: *time-dependent reaction*, *high-resolution image*, *reversed-phase chromatography*. Adverbs ending in *-ly* do not take a connecting hyphen: *rapidly measured response*. A numeral followed by an abbreviated unit is unhyphenated even before a noun: *a 10 mg sample*. Foreign phrases such as *in vitro* and *in situ* are also unhyphenated as modifiers. The chapter gives additional rules for chemical names, proper names, spelled-out numbers, and compound modifiers. (pp. 135–143)
- **Capitalize numbered elements and proper names, not generic terms.** Write *Figure 2*, *Table 1*, and *Scheme 3* when referring to specific items, but *the figure* or *the scheme* generically. Most chemical names and generic instrument names remain lowercase in running text. The 2006 guide uses title case for major words in titles, headings, and table/scheme/chart titles; scientific notation such as *pH* retains its required case. (pp. 143–151)
- **Use italics selectively.** The first occurrence of a newly defined term may be italicized, as are publication titles and biological genus/species names. Common Latin expressions such as *in vivo*, *in vitro*, *in situ*, and *et al.* remain roman. *pH* remains roman; the guide also distinguishes italic gene names from roman protein names. Greek symbols, rather than spelled-out names, are generally used for technical terms and remain roman. (pp. 153–155)
- **Introduce abbreviations deliberately.** Define a useful nonstandard abbreviation at first mention in the main text; if it appears in the abstract, define it separately there. Avoid unnecessary abbreviations in titles and avoid using one abbreviation for two different terms. Some familiar abbreviations, including DNA, RNA, IR, NMR, UV, and standard unit symbols, do not require definition under this guide. Case can change the meaning of a symbol, so preserve forms such as *mg*, *M*, and *pH*. The guide restricts *e.g.*, *i.e.*, *vs*, and *etc.* to parenthetical text, captions, and tables; spell them out in ordinary prose. (pp. 157–161)
- **Prefer generic product names when practical.** Trademarks are capitalized and function as modifiers of a generic noun. The appendices provide reference lists for computer terms and technical abbreviations rather than rules that need to be memorized. (pp. 157, 163–202)

**In practice:** Standardize compound names, figure references, abbreviation definitions, and recurring modifiers throughout the manuscript. The guide's examples illustrate its editorial conventions; a target journal's own house style may differ.

## Chapter 15 — Figures

### Extracted guidance

- **Choose a figure for a visual point.** Graphs communicate trends and comparisons at a glance; tables are better for exact values. Keep only figures that clarify or substantiate the discussion. Avoid crowding a figure, duplicating the same data in multiple places, or presenting something inconsistent with the text. Use color when it adds information that cannot be communicated clearly through line styles, symbols, or grayscale. (pp. 343–345)
- **Call out every figure in order.** Mention each numbered figure in the text. Use Arabic numerals for figures and a consistent labeling scheme for panels: for example, *Figure 2a,b* or *Figure 2A,B*. Do not mix an unlettered *Figure 2* with a separately designated *Figure 2A* in the same multipart figure. Structures and schemes have their own numbering sequences. (pp. 345–346)
- **Design at the final published size.** The chapter distinguishes line art, halftones such as photographs, grayscale line art, and combinations of images and type. The **2006 ACS guidance** specifies 1200 ppi for line art, 600 ppi for grayscale art and combinations, and 300 ppi for photographs or color halftones. It recommends crisp, adequately thick lines, readable fonts and symbols, and embedded fonts in suitable vector files. Simply changing the ppi setting of a small image does not restore missing detail. These technical figures describe the supplied edition's guidance, not a verified current requirement for any particular journal. (pp. 347–363)
- **Keep plots legible and economical.** Use distinguishable lines or markers and clearly identify all curves; the guide suggests splitting a plot with more than five curves. Label each axis with the measured quantity and units, use consistent capitalization and tick marks, avoid excessive blank space, and size the axes to the data. Captions can carry long explanations or symbol keys that would clutter the artwork. (pp. 355–360)
- **Make captions stand alone.** Begin with the figure number and a short informative description, then give conditions, panel descriptions, symbols, and abbreviations needed to interpret the figure independently. Use parallel wording in related captions. Check that the caption agrees with the artwork and the text. The source describes captions as separate from the artwork in production. (pp. 365–366)
- **Credit reused material.** The guide calls for written permission and a credit line when reproducing or adapting previously published figures or tables, subject to the cases it describes. An original new figure made from previously published data still needs a citation to the data source. (pp. 366–367)

**In practice:** For a multipart results figure, make panel letters, units, treatment names, and legend entries consistent across the graphic, caption, and main text. Identify the relevant experimental conditions in the caption so a reader can understand each panel on its own.

## Chapter 16 — Tables

### Extracted guidance

- **Use a table for precise, interrelated information.** Tables are particularly helpful when readers need many exact numbers or need to compare related variables. Avoid repeating entire tables in prose or figures. Very small sets of values may be clearer in a sentence. The guide distinguishes short, untitled in-text tables from numbered formal tables, for which it suggests at least three related columns and three rows as a design rule of thumb. (pp. 369–370)
- **Cite and number tables in discussion order.** Mention every table by its number in the text. A formal table has a brief informative title beginning with *Table* and its number; keep detailed qualifications in the footnotes. (pp. 369–371)
- **Give every column a meaningful heading.** Include the measured variable as well as its unit. Define nonstandard abbreviations in a footnote. Use parallel wording and consistent terms across related tables. Consider changing rows and columns if that improves legibility, and remove columns that contain mostly blanks or repeat a single value. (pp. 370–373)
- **Align entries for comparison.** Align words consistently, numerical values by decimal place when appropriate, and use a leading zero for values below one (*0.5*, not *.5*). Repeat an identical entry rather than using ditto marks. Explain a dash or other missing-value symbol in a footnote. (pp. 371–373)
- **Use footnotes for qualifications.** General footnotes describe conditions, units shared by the table, abbreviations, or common data sources; entry-specific footnotes explain exceptional values, conditions, or statistical marks. When lettered markers are used, the guide specifies lowercase italic superscripts in order from top to bottom and left to right; each footnote needs a matching callout. (p. 373)
- **Build actual table cells.** In a word processor, use its table feature instead of tabs and keep one row of entries per table row. The source also describes manuscript-placement and submission conventions that depend on the publisher. (pp. 373–374)

**In practice:** A compound comparison table should let a reader identify the property, unit, treatment condition, and provenance of each value without searching the Results section.

## Chapter 17 — Chemical Structures

### Extracted guidance

- **Draw structures where connectivity matters.** A simple compound may be communicated sufficiently by name or formula; a complex or newly reported structure benefits from a drawing that makes connectivity, bond order, and stereochemistry explicit. Chemical reactions can appear as individually numbered equations. A *chart* groups structures; a *scheme* groups reactions and shows a sequence or process. (pp. 375–376)
- **Keep numbering systems separate.** Assign consistent labels to compounds and use those labels in the prose; the chapter presents boldface numerals or letters as its convention for compound identifiers. Charts and schemes each have their own sequential numbers, separate from figure numbers and from the numbering of individual reactions. Do not draw compounds that never enter the discussion. (pp. 376–378)
- **Use a consistent drawing style.** Arrange structures in horizontal rows; keep ring, bond, type, and label sizes proportional. Center each compound label below its structure. For a related series, a common parent structure plus clearly specified variable substituents can reduce repetition. Tables can cite those structure labels instead of embedding large drawings in cells. (pp. 378–379)
- **Make reaction schemes easy to follow.** Center reaction arrows vertically relative to the structures; align components, leave balanced spacing around plus signs and arrows, and normally read from left to right. Use the available column width without excessive blank space. Avoid vertical arrows or two-column-wide schemes unless the chemistry requires them. The chapter gives historical ChemDraw parameter settings and reminds authors to check the intended publication's specifications. (pp. 379–382)

**In practice:** Use a scheme for a synthetic route or reaction sequence, a chart for a set of structures without reaction arrows, and a figure for analytical data or other illustrations. Preserve one compound-labeling convention across the molecular-design graphic, synthetic scheme, and Results text.

## Quick manuscript check

1. Are subjects, verbs, introductory modifiers, and restrictive clauses unambiguous? (Chapter 9)
2. Are abbreviations defined at first use, and are capitalization and compound modifiers consistent? (Chapter 10)
3. Does every figure or table appear in the text in numerical order, with a caption or title that can be understood independently? (Chapters 15–16)
4. Are plot axes labeled with units, and can all panels and conditions be distinguished at final print size? (Chapter 15)
5. Are compounds, reactions, charts, schemes, and figures labeled in coherent **separate** sequences? (Chapters 15 and 17)
6. Do chemical structures accurately show the relevant bond connectivity and align cleanly in the scheme? (Chapter 17)

**Scope note:** These are the conventions in the supplied **2006, third-edition** guide. Its named submission portals, file workflows, software examples, and numerical production specifications should be treated as historical guidance; the selected journal's author instructions govern the submitted manuscript.
